# METHOD

Working method drawn from Manfred Maier, *Basic Principles of Design* (Basel School of Design Foundation Program, Van Nostrand Reinhold, 1977, four volumes).

## 1. What this is

This is the working method of the Basel foundation year as Maier's four volumes record it. It was built from a complete read of all 432 pages, one reader per volume, every page logged. It describes how to work (how to limit, vary, compare, judge and refine). Nothing in it should be turned into a visual style.

**Citation key.** (V4 p.51) means Volume 4, page 51 on the reader site. See section 8.

---

## 2. The method in one page

The one place the book lists its process step by step is the Graphic Exercises course (V4 p.44). The loop below follows that list. The textile cycle (V3 p.44) and the memory-drawing cycle (V2 p.13) run the same way.

**Standing rules at every step**
- Work from the whole form to the related details (V1 p.12; V4 p.51).
- Question every result. There is "no single, absolute goal" (V1 p.12).
- When a piece goes stiff or wrong, make it again. Do not erase it or keep working it (V1 p.68; V2 p.79; V3 p.87; V4 p.70).

**The loop**
1. **Set a clear starting point.** Write the limits down before making anything: format, tools, colours, elements. (V4 p.13, 44, 45; V3 p.76; V2 p.13)
2. **Make many different trials.** At least 15–20 solutions that differ in kind. Variations of one solution do not count. (V4 p.46; V2 p.13; V3 p.13)
3. **Sort them and choose one clear theme.** Discuss the unsuitable ones. Take the most unequivocal as the form model. (V4 p.45, 46, 62; V3 p.44)
4. **Vary the theme.** At least 30 variations of the model. (V4 p.48; V3 p.44)
5. **Name criteria from the pieces and order them.** Sequence the pieces by each criterion, then build one horizontal-vertical structure. Gaps mark missing pieces. No likes or dislikes. (V4 p.20, 50, 51)
6. **Choose one row or direction.** It is still rough at this point. (V4 p.51)
7. **Refine by relating.** One change at a time across the whole row, each compared with the previous row. Never add pieces to cover a flaw. (V4 p.51, 63, 65, 66)
8. **Carry it to a new goal or medium.** Reinterpret its essentials at the new size or in the new material. The phases now run in parallel. (V4 p.44, 70; V3 p.90, 100; V2 p.13)

**On order.** "Limits, then whole before detail, then variations" is close but not exact. In the book, whole-before-detail governs every piece and phase, and only becomes a stage at the end, when the rough row is refined (V4 p.51, 62).

## 3. The principles in full

Each principle has a statement, then **What the book says** (paraphrase with citations), then **In practice** (my translation to screens, posts and sites). Practice steps that go beyond the text are marked *(interpretation)*.

### P1. Set the limits first and write them down

Fix the format, tools, materials, colours and number of elements before making anything. Remove every variable you are not studying.

**What the book says.**
- Every course opens with fixed starting conditions:
  - Graphic Exercises: four equal black strips inside a square as wide as all four, the strips kept inside. Tools are black paper, knife, scissors and glue (V4 p.45).
  - Color 1: a 9×9 cm square divided only vertically, so composition is not confused with colour. Mixing is limited to three pure colours with black and white (V4 p.13, 18), and the format is kept across exercises (V4 p.20).
  - Memory drawing: two pure colours plus black and white at first (V2 p.13).
  - Object drawing: one paper size, soft pencils, no shading (V1 p.13).
- Objectivity is reached "within clearly defined limitations" (V4 p.13).
- Colour is removed so that material can be seen (V3 p.18, 28, 30, 45). In lettering, word spaces and text content are set aside so attention goes to the surface (V2 p.87, 90).
- Limits are set fresh for each exercise by teacher and student together. Advanced students set their own (V3 p.76).
- A limit is a starting frame. The nine-section grid may be expanded when new possibilities appear (V3 p.14).
- Technique is limited because developing the process takes long (V4 p.45). When the options outrun the time, the student plans a limited program, and the teacher advises that planning is needed (V4 p.36).

**In practice.**
- Put a limitation statement at the top of the task: sizes, typefaces and weights, colour count, elements, fixed copy, tools, timebox.
- Lock what is not under study. For a layout task, work in greyscale with real copy. For a colour task, freeze the layout. *(interpretation)*
- Write new limits for each task. If a limit blocks a real discovery, widen it on purpose and record the change.

### P2. Simple to complex, one new factor at a time

Master the elementary case, then add one factor. Defer hard material until its prerequisites hold.

**What the book says.**
- Each exercise is the prerequisite for the next (V2 p.12).
- The cube takes the first weeks and simple solids the first months (V1 p.13, 15, 20).
- Color 1 analyses kinds of contrast, then adds quantity as a new factor (V4 p.32). One added horizontal division multiplies the possibilities (V4 p.42). Complexity rises in named steps: irregular values, unequal stripes, unlimited colours (V4 p.18).
- Shadow constructions and hard penetrations are deferred "at this stage" (V2 p.51, 59).
- Craft comes first: correct paint application before expression (V3 p.77), tool handling before construction (V2 p.47), thread unchanged before altered (V3 p.50).

**In practice.**
- Solve one breakpoint, static, before adding motion, a second breakpoint or imagery.
- Add one named factor per round.
- Keep a deferred list that says what is held back and when it comes in.

### P3. Whole before detail

Establish the total form first. Details are subordinate and come last.

**What the book says.**
- Volume 1 defines its method as developing from the total form to related details (V1 p.12), beginning with light, free lines (V1 p.13). The draughtsman is like a carver finding the form in a rough block (V1 p.27).
- Details are always seen in relation to the whole, so the basic form stays dominant (V1 p.38, 66). Broom bristles are drawn as one blunt cone (V1 p.28).
- In memory drawing, composition is fixed in pencil before colour (V2 p.13), and details differentiate only in the enlargement (V2 p.40).
- In Graphic Exercises, formal and rhythmic aspects are secondary early. Intervals and details are refined only in phase 4 (V4 p.51, 62).
- Harmonies are compared only as wholes. Detailed analysis of three-colour relations is called too complex and not useful (V4 p.20).
- A large subject demands placing it well in the format and omitting the inessential (V1 p.72).

**In practice.**
- Start each screen as blocks of tone and size. Type styling, icons and effects wait until the blocks hold.
- Judge each variation whole, at thumbnail size, before looking at parts.
- If a detail pulls attention from the whole, subordinate it or cut it.

### P4. Many trials that differ in kind; keep the failures

Produce many genuinely different solutions before choosing. Unclear and wrong attempts are part of the work.

**What the book says.**
- Phase 1 asks for at least 15–20 different solutions (V4 p.46), phase 2 for at least 30 variations (V4 p.48). Colour students paint 30–40 squares, then 40–60 (V4 p.14, 20). Memory drawing starts from many small sketches at 10.5 × 14.8 cm (V2 p.13). Material Studies wants "as many variations as possible" (V3 p.13).
- Variations on one solution do not count as different solutions (V4 p.46).
- Skill and exactness come from the number of pieces (V4 p.45).
- False or unclear solutions are fundamental to understanding the process (V4 p.46). Unsuitable attempts are printed (V4 p.45), beginners' mistakes are named (V1 p.15) and expected (V1 p.18), and initial difficulties are shown (V2 p.104).

**In practice.**
- Make phase 1 small and rough so that many is cheap.
- Ask of each trial: a new solution, or a variant of one already here? Merge variants.
- Keep rejected trials in the record with a one-line reason.

### P5. Vary by holding constants and moving parts

Change one variable while the others are held. Keep parts movable so arrangements can be tried without remaking.

**What the book says.**
- One colour is held while two change in equal-looking steps (V4 p.26). Rows change colour only, proportion only, then both (V4 p.28, 30, 32). A fixed pair sits between changing neighbours to isolate simultaneous contrast (V4 p.24). Chosen colours are remixed in jars so they stay identical (V4 p.22, 32).
- Paper is used because pieces can be swapped and shifted, which allows a flexible, comparative method (V4 p.45). Threads, wood blocks and collage pieces are moved, turned and exchanged to make new versions (V3 p.26, 40, 94).
- The same knot in different materials makes material the variable (V3 p.57). The same finished piece under different light or grounds looks different (V3 p.19–22, 32, 36).
- Changes should be intentional, never random (V4 p.36).

**In practice.**
- Build variations from shared components or tokens so one value changes and all else stays identical.
- For each round, write "held: … / varied: …".
- Show one design in changed conditions (light and dark mode, small phone, wide monitor, feed thumbnail) as a variation in its own right.

### P6. Criteria come from the work, fresh each time

Find criteria by laying out and comparing the actual pieces, and name them. No fixed standard carries over between tasks.

**What the book says.**
- Criteria are defined according to the actual working situation (V4 p.44), on the basis of spontaneous experiments (V4 p.46).
- Volume 3 redefines criteria in each exercise by comparing results (V3 p.12, 76).
- Phase-3 criteria came from the pieces: light-dark, static-dynamic, simple-complex, one-several, few-many, all treated as equivalent (V4 p.50).
- Color 1 starts with three questions per pair: a hue difference? a value difference? a brightness difference? (V4 p.14)
- Experimental results set new goals for faculty too (V3 p.12). The designer must keep questioning existing structures and functions (V4 p.12).
- Partial exception: lettering works from one shared model and stays close to the standard (V2 p.87). See section 7.

**In practice.**
- After phase 2, lay out every variation and write three to six two-ended criteria that actually separate them (dense–open, one–many focal points, quiet–tense).
- Criteria describe what is visible. "Modern", "premium" and "on brand" are not criteria in this sense. *(interpretation)*
- Re-derive the list on every project.

### P7. Order everything; gaps are information

Every piece goes into one ordered structure. Nothing is dropped for being disliked. Empty places show what is missing.

**What the book says.**
- Like and dislike are excluded; every square goes into the organization, grouped from minimal to very large difference (V4 p.20). Every piece is equally important (V4 p.50).
- Sorting by one criterion shows the student what determines his pieces. Rows are then combined into a readable horizontal-vertical structure (V4 p.50).
- Overlaps and gaps are read as blanks for pieces still missing. A chance branching layout is a contradiction (V4 p.50).
- The smallest interval between two pieces sets the grid size, which stays open in every direction. Pieces that will not fit get extra relating planes (V4 p.51).
- Ordering takes many time-consuming trials. First attempts frustrate everyone, and each trial organization is kept as a sketch (V4 p.51).
- Pieces off the theme are flagged as variations of a different solution (V4 p.58, 64).

**In practice.**
- Use the board in section 6. Place every variation, label off-theme ones, leave gaps empty and labelled, save each board version.

### P8. Choose one clear theme or direction

Choose for clarity. A loose choice causes trouble later.

**What the book says.**
- The student compares, decides which designs are clear, and picks one as the form model (V4 p.46). The aim is an unequivocal theme (V4 p.62).
- Choosing two different processes as the theme makes the next phase harder and leaves pieces out (V4 p.46, 52). A borderline solution is named and not chosen (V4 p.46).
- One row is then chosen and completed in relation to the whole (V4 p.51).
- In memory drawing the choice is made with the teacher (V2 p.13) and repeated at every stage: pencil, colour, enlargement (V2 p.18, 20, 28, 30, 31, 33). Selection can reward working method, such as a spontaneous approach (V2 p.25).

**In practice.**
- The agent proposes; the owner chooses *(interpretation of V2 p.13)*. The agent brings the ordered board and says which candidates are clear, which borderline, and why.
- Ask "which is clearest for the stated purpose?" Never ask "which do you like?"
- Choose again at each stage: structure, colour, each new medium.

### P9. Refine by relating, one change at a time, nothing added

Refinement comes last. Change one relationship across the whole, compare with the previous state, and never cover a flaw with a new element.

**What the book says.**
- Phase 4 refines intervals, details and pieces on logical and qualitative grounds (V4 p.51).
- Changes are made one at a time across the whole row, each compared with the original (V4 p.51). Each derived row changes one relationship (V4 p.66).
- Imperfections may not be fixed by adding pieces. Quality comes from relating existing pieces to each other and to the whole (V4 p.51).
- Three pieces are the smallest unit for judging interval: 1 to 2 as 2 to 3, and to the whole (V4 p.51).
- The clearest row relates every formal element in every piece (V4 p.60); rows are judged on consistent progression (V4 p.54). Steps too similar to their neighbours are removed (V4 p.88).
- New elements must fit existing relationships and keep the original divisions readable (V4 p.40). Decisions are only ever met in relationships (V4 p.26).

**In practice.**
- Keep the refinement log in section 6. Judge spacing, size and tone steps in threes.
- If the design feels weak, change a relationship (ratio, interval, tone step) before considering any new element.
- Remove near-duplicate steps, such as two type sizes or two greys too close together.

### P10. The eye decides; aids check it

What looks equal is the target. Grids and tests control the eye, and fall away as judgement grows. Intuition alone fails at refinement.

**What the book says.**
- Series steps must look equal whether or not they measure equal (V4 p.26). Lettering aims at optically equal spaces, and the closed O is drawn smaller (V2 p.79, 87). Parallel changes do not move in step, so conflicting criteria are weighed (V4 p.34).
- Students rarely manage proportion without grids (V4 p.32, 36). A row adjusted purely by intuition came out uneven (V4 p.54).
- Checks recur: a circle inserted in a cube face (V1 p.16, 46), a small profile view in the sheet's corner (V1 p.17), diagonals (V2 p.50), finding a third view from two (V2 p.56), building a model (V2 p.56, 74).
- Distance is a check: standing to write and sloped boards let large forms be seen from further away (V2 p.88, 96).
- Aids are temporary. Drawing negative spaces is a crutch that experience removes (V1 p.30). Lettering bans helping lines so spacing stays optical (V2 p.79).

**In practice.**
- Set proportions with a grid and spacing scale, then correct by eye where equal measures look unequal (round letters, icons, ragged edges).
- View at 25% and from across the room. Blur the screen; cover one area and see if the rest holds.

### P11. Judge under fair conditions

Compare like with like, on neutral ground, beside an unworked reference, and check the light-dark structure in grey.

**What the book says.**
- Wet tempera cannot be compared. Test on a separate sheet; only dried colour gives a true comparison (V4 p.26–27).
- Use good light and a neutral grey background (V4 p.35).
- One burlap section is left unworked as the reference for every treatment (V3 p.28).
- A collage is put under glass to remove curl and shadow, and paint is tried on the glass before committing (V3 p.98).
- Grey-value studies come before colour, to understand the composition's light-dark effect (V2 p.13, 18). Colour is translated into grey tones (V1 p.100).
- The object stand neutralizes the surroundings (V1 p.21).

**In practice (the grey check).**
- Compare at the same size, in the same frame, with real fonts and copy. A half-edited mockup is wet paint. *(interpretation)*
- Keep a control beside every round: the live version, or a plain unstyled one.
- Desaturate each candidate. Hierarchy must read in tone alone; if it depends on hue, fix tone first.
- View on a neutral mid-grey surround. Try additions on a duplicate.

### P12. Redo; do not erase or overwork

When a piece goes wrong or stiff, make it again from the chosen state. Keep the evidence of process.

**What the book says.**
- Long work on one drawing loses spontaneity. Repeated studies give a clearer formulation (V1 p.68).
- Lettering is corrected without erasing: the exercise is repeated so forms can be compared (V2 p.79), developing through continual redrawing (V2 p.78).
- A clear solution often means repainting an area or repeating the whole work (V3 p.87). In ink a line cannot be moved, so the work is repeated (V4 p.70).
- Construction lines are kept as evidence (V1 p.13); a good study shows its search for form (V1 p.79).
- Correction differs by medium: knife or blade, never opaque paint, in ink (V2 p.48); only added glazes in aquarelle, so lights are planned from the start (V3 p.90); sandpaper on dried paint (V3 p.28).

**In practice.**
- After two or three patches, regenerate the piece from its last good state.
- Never overwrite. Save numbered versions or commits.
- Plan early whatever is hard to undo, such as the main open space and the focal point. *(interpretation of V3 p.90)*

### P13. Use chance, then control it

Play and accident produce what planning cannot foresee. Notice them, then shape them consciously.

**What the book says.**
- Unbiased experiment and noticing chance give possibilities the intellect cannot foresee (V3 p.13). Contrasts found through play are then consciously formulated (V3 p.22).
- Work comes from "spontaneous but controlled actions" (V3 p.29); controlling chance needs quick reactions (V3 p.42). Collage is "controlled play" (V3 p.94). Free brushwork needs concentration and control (V3 p.82).
- Unexpected effects are taken in (V3 p.87); a tearing accident becomes a formal element (V3 p.95). Chance wrinkling gives raw material, then conscious ordering follows (V4 p.83).
- The weight of spontaneity varies: subordinated in object drawing (V1 p.13), small mistakes accepted if lively in museum drawing (V1 p.54), personal expression judged in nature drawing (V1 p.76).

**In practice.**
- Generate loosely in phase 1. Mark any accident worth keeping and describe it in formal terms. It then goes on the board like everything else.
- State in the limitation statement how much weight spontaneity gets in this project. *(interpretation)*

### P14. Compare across people; hand over responsibility; give it time

Shared starting conditions make results comparable across people. The teacher frames and advises; the student analyses and gradually takes over decisions. Phases are paced together.

**What the book says.**
- Comparison: a group's range of solutions exceeds what one person could foresee (V4 p.12), and another's result can open a new direction (V4 p.48). Three students' sequences from the same conditions are shown side by side (V4 p.45, 68), as are many students' works on one theme (V2 p.16, 19, 41; V1 p.82). Students learn their decisions depend on concept, criteria and organization (V4 p.68). Comparative control builds independent judgement (V2 p.12); self-criticism grows with involvement (V3 p.76).
- Roles: the teacher supplies themes (V2 p.13), explains at the blackboard (V4 p.54), circulates suggesting corrections (V2 p.28), suggests choices in doubtful cases while the student analyses (V4 p.14), and advises on planning while the student builds the program (V4 p.36). Critique gets the student to the goal faster (V1 p.65).
- Responsibility shifts: the student must find the material and process (V3 p.22), later chooses freely (V3 p.34, 42), and when advanced sets the limits (V3 p.76).
- Time: one year at 42 hours a week (V1 p.7). Everyone finishes each phase in about the same time so the group can compare (V4 p.45), though personal rhythm varies (V4 p.40). The process is slow (V4 p.45, 51). Quick-drying paint supports fast iteration (V3 p.28).

**In practice.**
- The owner sets limits and theme and makes the choices. The agent produces, orders, analyses and logs. *(interpretation)*
- On important tasks, have two agents (or two people) work the same limitation statement independently, then compare boards.
- Timebox each phase and finish it everywhere before comparing.
- As trust grows, let an agent set its own limits within a stated frame.

### P15. Carry over by reinterpreting

A new size, medium or goal is a new design problem. Translate the essentials.

**What the book says.**
- Learning is meant to transfer to other media and complex problems (V4 p.12, 44). The strip model moves to ruled ink, where the phases run in parallel (V4 p.70).
- Enlargement needs remixed colour, because a colour reads differently when its quantity changes, and spontaneity is often lost (V2 p.13, 19).
- Collages are translated into tempera by reinterpreting their essential forms and colours, and a size change adds difficulty (V3 p.100). Translation needs an understanding of what each technique can and cannot do (V3 p.90).
- Recasting a form in another material needs new creative thinking (V4 p.76, 104). Abstracted forms keep their character (V4 p.101).

**In practice.**
- Desktop to mobile, site to post, post to print: each is a translation. Restate the limits and recheck the essentials.
- Recheck colour whenever an area changes size. A colour that works on a button can fail as a background.

## 5. Checklists

**Before you start**
- [ ] Limitation statement written: sizes, type, colours, elements, copy, tools, timebox. (P1)
- [ ] Variables not under study locked; deferred list written. (P1, P2)
- [ ] Starting questions named: what is under study, such as size ratio, position, density (V2 p.13; V4 p.14). Working criteria will be named later, from the pieces. (P6)
- [ ] Control version chosen. (P11)
- [ ] Roles set: who frames, who produces, who chooses. (P14)
- [ ] Phases timeboxed; a limited variation program planned if options outrun time. (P1, P14)

**Before you call it done**
- [ ] 15–20 distinct solutions, then 30+ variations, made. (P4)
- [ ] Every variation on the board; off-theme pieces flagged; gaps labelled. (P7)
- [ ] Choice made for clarity, reason recorded. (P8)
- [ ] Refinement log shows one change per step against the previous state, and nothing added to cover a flaw. (P9)
- [ ] Intervals checked in threes; near-duplicate steps removed; every element relates to the whole. (P9)
- [ ] Whole reads before details. (P3)
- [ ] Grey check passed against the control, same size, real rendering, neutral ground. (P11)
- [ ] Viewed small and from a distance. (P10)
- [ ] Each new size or medium reinterpreted and its colour rechecked. (P15)
- [ ] All states saved. (P12)
- [ ] Result questioned: one answer to these limits, open to the next. (P6)

## 6. Applying it to a project: homepage hero

An agent told "follow METHOD.md" runs a new design task like this. Values are examples.

**6.1 Limitation statement** (P1, P2, P13, P14)
```
TASK        Homepage hero
FRAME       1440 x 800 desktop. Mobile later, as a translation.
ELEMENTS    Headline (fixed copy), one supporting line, one button. No image.
TYPE        One family, two weights.
COLOUR      Black, white, one grey until the grey check passes.
UNDER STUDY Relation of headline, line and button in the frame: size, position, interval.
LOCKED      Copy, typeface.
DEFERRED    Imagery, motion, mobile, brand colour (each enters later as one factor).
QUESTIONS   Size ratio; position in format; density; number of focal points.
CONTROL     Screenshot of the current hero.
SPONTANEITY High in phase 1, low in phase 4.
ROLES       Owner frames and chooses. Agent produces, orders, logs.
TIME        One session per phase.
```

**6.2 Variations** (P4, P5)
- Phase 1: 15–20 structurally different solutions as small greyscale thumbnails with real copy. Shifting a block is a variant and does not count. Unsuitable ones stay in, with a note. One form model is chosen with the owner.
- Phase 2: 30+ variations of the model, each with "held / varied" noted, made by moving parts of the model.

**6.3 The board** (P6, P7)
1. Lay all phase-2 pieces out. Write the criteria they actually differ on.
2. Sort the pieces into one row per criterion. Save each.
3. Pick two criteria as axes. Cell size = the smallest visible step between two pieces.
4. Place every piece. Empty cells stay empty and labelled. Pieces that will not place go in a side column, "other solution".
5. Save each trial board. Muddled early boards are expected.

```
           static ........................ dynamic
small    [07] [12] [ missing ] [03]
         [22] [09] [15]        [28]
large    [ missing ] [01] [19] [30]
other solution: 11, 24 (two themes mixed)
```

**6.4 How the owner chooses** (P8)
The agent sends the board, the named criteria, which rows read most clearly, which candidates are borderline and why, and the labelled gaps. The owner picks one row, column or piece as the direction. The agent records the choice and the reason in criteria terms.

**6.5 Refinement log** (P9, P12)

| # | One change | Compared with | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Chosen piece as is | control | baseline | |
| 1 | Headline-to-line size ratio 3:1 → 4:1 | #0 | kept | headline, line, button now step evenly as three |
| 2 | Line-to-button gap 24 → 40 | #1 | reverted | button detaches; whole weakens |
| 3 | Headline onto first column edge | #1 | kept | one edge now relates all parts to the nav |

Everything not named in the row is held. If a change only works once something is added, revert it. If the piece has gone stiff, remake it from the last kept row.

**6.6 Grey check** (P10, P11)
1. Render the last kept state and the control at the same size with real fonts.
2. Desaturate both and set them on a neutral mid-grey surround.
3. Confirm the hierarchy reads in tone alone: focal point, then line, then button.
4. View at 25% and slightly blurred. The structure must survive.
5. Then add brand colour as one new factor with its own small variation round (P2), and translate to mobile under restated limits (P15).

## 7. Open questions

**Disagreements between volumes or courses**
- **Objective or subjective colour.** Color 1 replaces taste with objective knowledge (V4 p.13). Color 2 treats colour as subjective expression (V3 p.76), and nature drawing is judged on personal interpretation (V1 p.76). Volume 3 says its goals become clear alongside Volume 4's structured courses (V3 p.12). The book never reconciles them directly.
- **Grids and helping lines.** Lettering bans helping lines (V2 p.79). Color 1 says students rarely manage without grids (V4 p.32, 36), technical drawing permits tracing grids (V2 p.69), and a memory sketch is refined over a grid (V2 p.30).
- **How to correct.** Blade, never opaque paint (V2 p.48); repeat, never erase (V2 p.79; V4 p.70); add glazes only (V3 p.90); sand dried paint (V3 p.28).
- **Fixed standard.** Lettering keeps close to a shared standard (V2 p.87). Elsewhere there is no absolute goal (V1 p.12) and criteria are redefined each exercise (V3 p.12).
- **Who selects and judges.** With the teacher in memory drawing (V2 p.13); the student in Graphic Exercises (V4 p.46), with teacher suggestions only in doubtful cases (V4 p.14). No volume says who gives the final evaluation, and no grading is described.
- **Decoration.** Ruled out (V4 p.45; V3 p.44) yet allowed as contrast in nature drawing (V1 p.76).
- **Spontaneity.** Subordinated (V1 p.13), central (V3 p.13), and shown failing alone in refinement (V4 p.54).
- **"Not systematic."** Volume 3 says its exercises are not arranged systematically (V3 p.12), yet its courses follow a clear order (V3 p.45, 50, 53, 76). The reader takes it to mean no theoretical system.
- **"Linear."** It means simple to difficult (V1 p.7) and also whole form to details (V1 p.12).

**Text against plates**
- The aquarelle-to-tempera plates flatten soft gradients into four or five values (V3 p.93). The text does not name simplification (V3 p.90).
- The plate shows a sketch refined over a grid, but the text does not describe the grid (V2 p.30).
- Four shoe stages are promised but the scan shows three views on one sheet (V1 p.40). A six-piece row is described but the scan shows two before and two after (V4 p.54).
- The "three-dimensional" organization appears as overlaid planes on a flat grid (V4 p.51).
- One square of the nine-section paper may be an unworked control, but the text is silent (V3 p.14).
- Plates on V4 p.71–75 and V3 p.101–107 have no captions.

**Other uncertainties**
- Shading is ruled out (V1 p.13) but light tone is allowed (V1 p.37, 55).
- Lettering lists capitals before curves (V2 p.78) but teaches curves first (V2 p.84–87).
- "Two pure colours" (V2 p.13) becomes "two bright colours" in captions.
- No exercise durations are given beyond weeks and months (V1 p.13, 15). Timetable hours cannot be matched to courses (V3 p.10), and course order within the year is unstated.
- The source text is column-scrambled on V1 p.7, 12, 13; V2 p.12, 14; V3 p.7; V4 p.12, 20, 26, 32, 36, 40, 45. Check the scan before quoting from these pages.
